Three problems to model

The calculus in all three is one line. The work is in the modelling: pick one variable, use the constraint to kill the other, write the objective, then differentiate. Pick a problem and the lab plots your objective function over its feasible range and marks the stationary point it finds.

Convex here, concave there

Convexity is a statement about f″, so the whole question is: where is f″ ≥ 0? For all three functions the answer comes down to the sign of a quadratic. The plot shades the convex region, and the readout reports the switch points it computed from your parameters.

f f″ convex (f″ > 0)

A second derivative is a sign question, not a number question. You are never asked for the value of f″, only for where it changes sign. So simplify until you have something positive times something whose sign you can read — a square, an exponential, or q² in a denominator — and then only the second factor matters. Both the log and the exponential case below reduce to exactly that.

When the second derivative says nothing

For f(x) = −(x − 4)⁴ the first derivative vanishes at x* = 4 — and so does the second, and the third. The second-derivative test is silent. Step up through the derivatives and watch where the first non-zero one appears.

The general rule

Suppose f′(x*) = f″(x*) = … = f⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(x*) = 0 and f⁽ᵏ⁾(x*) ≠ 0. Taylor's theorem says that near x*,

f(x* + h) ≈ f(x*) + f(k)(x*)·hk/k!

and the sign of hᵏ decides everything:

kf(k)(x*)VerdictWhy
even< 0local maximumhᵏ > 0 on both sides, so the correction is negative on both sides.
even> 0local minimumSame, with the correction positive on both sides.
odd≠ 0neither — an inflectionhᵏ flips sign with h, so f rises on one side and falls on the other.

The familiar second-derivative test is just the case k = 2. It is not a different rule, only the first one you meet.

And a shortcut worth noticing. Here f″(x) = −12(x − 4)² ≤ 0 for every x, so f is concave on all of ℝ. A stationary point of a concave function is a global maximum, so that single observation settles the problem without any Taylor expansion. The distinction is worth being precise about: the second-derivative test at the point is inconclusive because f″(4) = 0, while the global sign of f″ is conclusive. Both facts come from the same formula.

Four densities and their first two moments

Each function integrates to 1 over the region where it is non-zero — that is what makes it a density. Outside that region f = 0, so those parts of the integral contribute nothing and the infinite limits are harmless. The lab shades the area it is integrating and compares your closed forms against a numerical integral of the same formula.

Reading the three integrals

IntegralName later in BS1515What it tells you
∫f(x)dxtotal probabilityNothing about the distribution — it is a consistency check. If it is not 1, the function is not a density, or you have the wrong limits.
∫x·f(x)dxexpectation, ExThe balance point of the shaded area. Note it need not be where f peaks.
∫x2·f(x)dxsecond moment, Ex²On its own, little. Combined as Ex² − (Ex)² it is the variance — the spread.

A check you can always run. Ex² − (Ex)² is a variance, so it must come out positive. If your two moments give a negative answer, one of them is wrong — and you knew that before looking up the right answer. The readout computes it for you on every parameter change.

A second check: Ex must lie inside the range where f > 0. An expectation outside the support is impossible.
